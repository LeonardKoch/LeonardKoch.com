If controlled right, coding agents are a great [uplift in productivity for the skilled and an amazing enabler for the less experienced](/post/skill-gap-induced-ai-awe).
But what should we do with this gain?

How should we spend the agentic development dividend?

I think we should use it to ease the user experience by reducing its complexity.

{/* revise this sentence */}
As software craftspeople we're always battling the complexity in the problems we're solving and the products we're building to do it.
When we really understand the problem well, iterate through many different solutions and
find the right one we can give the user just the control they need and have everything else flow from that.

{/* revise this sentence */}
Other times we fall short of that and that work falls on the user.
May that be with overwrought forms, flows that don't align with the users mental model, or dozens of sliders and check-boxes that could have been much simpler.<br />
Where things end up is often up to us and how much work we're able and willing to spend.

The brilliant developer and experience designer [Larry Tesler](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Larry_Tesler) encapsulated
that tension in his [Law of the conservation of complexity](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Law_of_conservation_of_complexity).
>"...every application has an inherent amount of complexity that cannot be removed or hidden. Instead, it must be dealt with, either in product development or in user interaction."

A more practical perspective is that as a developer you have the opportunity to invest time and brain power,
so your users have less complexity to deal with.

<TeslersLawDiagram complexity={70} agent={false} />

Now - for the most part - that brain power has to come from us as developers.
{/* revise this sentence */}
Deeply understanding the complexities of the product, evaluating options to simplify it, the human perception of its different aspects
and choosing the right ones for its users is quite subjective and thus far beyond agents without a lot of intervention.

However we can be more daring and experiment more freely while exploring these different options
when the agent is doing most of the typing.

Maybe we'll get that green bar to be smaller over time, but for now this is as optimistic as I'm willing to get.

### Why focus on abstracting complexity?
In a world where software is abundant, differentiation lies in specific needs filled extremely well.
Those needs get filled by solving a problem and the more you can boil out the complexity for the user, the better.
In most cases this wins over writing more features because creating an abundance of functionality
is becoming easier and easier, while most of it goes unused by each individual user.
Whereas deeply solving a problem in a manner that addresses the users need is not becoming more common at all.
As proportion of all software it arguably becoming more rare.
Actually solving these hard problems gives you that differentiation and will allow your product to stand out
and win over time.
